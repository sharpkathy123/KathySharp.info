Stroke victims cheer a peer

Dave Hyde | Sports Columnist
February 1, 2008
PHOENIX

Tim Lange does not know Tedy Bruschi. He does not know the details of the New England linebacker's career, of Bruschi's five Pro Bowls, three Super Bowl rings or even that he starred at nearby Arizona.

But Lange knows something Bruschi embraces above all those titles.

"He sat in this same chair," Lange says.

Lange, 47, an electrical engineer, doesn't mean this specific chair, on the second floor of St. Joseph's. He means one just like it, in some other hospital, from which Bruschi fought the same battles as a stroke victim that Lange fights now.

Bruschi had to re-learn how to write.

"My handwriting sucks," Lange said.

Bruschi had to re-find his equilibrium.

"Each day I go out and walk on uneven surfaces, just to teach myself how to do it again," Lange said.

Bruschi couldn't see out of his left eye, then couldn't coordinate his two eyes.

"Part of my homework is to work on vision-related problems," Lange said. "I line up numbers and do math problems. I read letters around in a circle to see what word they make. It's all about regaining the coordination of your vision."

Most of all, best of all, Bruschi got the happy ending that Lange fights for since his Christmas Eve stroke. Bruschi regained his strength, recaptured his body's focus and reclaimed his life as a pro football player. This is why, as Bruschi runs out on the Super Bowl field Sunday, he'll do so with the joy of achievement.

"A stroke survivor in the Super Bowl," he said. "That means more to me than anything else."

Three years ago, two days after returning from the Pro Bowl, Bruschi woke up in the middle of the night in pain. His left leg gave out as he got out of bed. His body went numb. Within a few hours, he was lying in a hospital gurney and a doctor was delivering the news that couldn't be true:

"Tedy, you've had a stroke."

Thirty-one-year-olds don't have strokes, do they? Not when they're football players. Certainly not when they take care of themselves in the manner Bruschi did. At least that's what he thought.

"I didn't think I'd ever be a normal person again," he says. "Playing football wasn't even in the realm of possibility to me."

But as he started the road to rehabilitation, no therapist said his limbs wouldn't work well again. No doctor said he shouldn't play again. After 12 weeks of therapy, he began to believe, too. Day by day, step by step, he became who he was to the point that 10 months after the stroke he was on the field again.

"What I found was I wasn't alone in my comeback," he said.

Stroke victims reached out to him. First by the dozens. Then hundreds. Then thousands. Two youth-aged brothers in Boston who are stroke victims put a Bruschi poster on their bedroom wall. A Williams College swimmer raised money for a teammate who was a stroke victim. A high school hockey player. A school principal. A stockbroker.

You see this across sports, tragedy turning into inspiration. What Alonzo Mourning has done for kidney patients and Lance Armstrong for cancer patients, Bruschi has done for stroke victims. He has shown they can go on with their lives. He also has started Tedy's Team and so far raised $500,000 toward helping research.

"I think it happened to me for a reason," Bruschi said.

From his hospital room in downtown Phoenix, Lange talks of getting back his life. He was an active cyclist, hiker and rock climber when a spat of headaches began to worsen Christmas Eve. Doctors found a brain clot in the back of his head. He's been in the hospital since.

"I'm in better shape than a lot of people," he said. "You go in the gymnasium here and you see a lot of wheelchairs. And everyone wants the same thing, really. They want to get back to where they were."

Sunday, on the Super Bowl field, they'll see one of their community who did just that. And, as Lange says, in speaking for thousands, showing how even the biggest game isn't about winning and losing: "If he can do it, it makes you think, 'Why can't I?'"
