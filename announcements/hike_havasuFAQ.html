Quick Details

  1. Rules: To go on this trip you must read and agree to Havasu Canyon Rules and Regulations. You will need to sign these rules in the village of Supai. (More details are in the camping section of the full details.)
  2. Dates: The trip is four days.  We'll drive up Thursday night, hike down on Friday morning, May 17, 2013, and hike out on Monday morning, May 20, 2013.
  3. Camping fee: See here. Don't pay until Kathy asks, about a week before the trip.
  4. Pack horses:
    • Using pack horses is optional.
    • Each pack can weigh 30 pounds, and we'll put up to 4 packs or bags on each horse.
    • We have to rent whole horses, so we divide the price of the pack horses between those who use the horses. When we make the pack horse reservation, we'll put the price per bag on the trip fees page.
    • We have to reserve our pack horses one week before the trip. Before then, make sure your wishes are on our trip participant list.
    • Drop off packs for the horses at Hualapai Hilltop by 10 a.m. Friday and just outside the Havasupai campground gate by 7 a.m. Monday.
    • More details are in the pack horse section of the full details and in the FAQs on this page.
  5. Meeting place: TBD. See also the schedule estimate section of the full details.
  6. FRS radios: We'll be using channel 9, code 1.
  7. Helicopter: The helicopter company for 2007 is Air West (623-516-2790). The fee (as of 2/2/2013) for a  one-way ride is $85 per person with one backpack. More details are in the FAQs on this page.
  8. Phone: Tourist Office phone numbers are (928) 448-2141 or 2121. (These are the phone numbers Kathy uses to make camping and pack horse reservations. Note that the tribe often doesn't answer for days on end, even when they've told you specifically when to call.)
  9. Children: The tribe no longer gives discounts for kids.
  10. Hiking time:  A time estimate for the hike between Hualapai Hilltop and the campground is 4 to 5 hours either way for the average hiker.  (The optional hike between the campground and the river takes about 4 hours of fast hiking each way and is much more strenuous.)
  11. Trail mileage estimate:
    Hualapai Hilltop, our trailhead, to the Tourist Office in the village of Supai: 8 miles
    Supai to Havasu Falls and the campground: 2 miles
    Havasupai campground: half mile of the creek
    Optional hike from campground to Beaver Falls: 3 miles (one way)
    Optional hike from Beaver Falls to Colorado River: 5 miles (one way)

FAQs

What kind of bag should we get for the pack horse?

It's up to you, but 30 pounds is by far the easiest to carry as a backpack and hardest to carry in a huge, floppy bag. The pack horse bags get a very bumpy ride tied with rope onto a wood frame on a trotting horse's back. My canvas duffels have small holes worn into them from rides on the Havasupai pack horses.

I use ugly green canvas duffel bags (cheap copies of army stuff, probably) that only have a hole in one end. I figure that reduces the chance of busting a zipper and having things fall out, but there's just one hook holding it all closed, and we've had to repair even that. I don't think anyone has had a zipper burst open, although some people have put straps or tape around bags to prevent splitting open during the rough ride.

Our duffel bags are much, much easier to carry if the bag is stuffed real tight. You have to carry the bags from where the horses drop them off (at the gate to the campground) to the selected campsite, which can be quite a distance. My bigger bags hold more stuff, but they are so hard to carry that I really prefer having to work real hard to stuff everything into our smaller duffels.

I can't remember what kind of jacket I take into the campground.

Keep an eye on the Supai weather before the trip, but usually just a light fleece jacket in the evenings, along with long pants, is fine.  Also be prepared for rain, even if it is not in the forecast.

My biggest question is what to bring for food.

Try to keep it light in weight.  Here's what I plan on bringing; adjust to your tastes.

For the Thursday night drive, I bring a drink and sandwich to eat in the car or at our rim campsite (which has no facilities).  I sometimes bring extra breakfast stuff for the car camp morning (Friday), such as a Kern's fruit drink and a banana.

For the four breakfasts, I usually bring dry cereal and sometimes also eat some of my snacks.  (You could bring powdered milk for cereal.  You can boil water and pour it directly into instant oatmeal bags, too.)

For the four daily hiking snacks and lunches, I bring breadsticks (they get smashed but not stale), nuts, string cheese, oranges, peanut M&M's, and dried fruit.  (The cheese and oranges are my heaviest food items. Oranges are a good backpacking fruit because they can be abused.)  I might bring a boiled egg or two to eat near the beginning of the trip.

For the three dinners I bring Mountain House backpacker meals.  If the restaurant is operational, I sometimes have a meal there.  On occasion, I have purposely planned to eat at least one meal there.  (I usually bring too much food and have to pack some out, but I'm so paranoid about getting stuck in the middle of nowhere.)

I usually bring a large quantity of Gator Ade, since I get tired of drinking plain water.  Note that alcohol is ILLEGAL on the reservation.

Also, we often stop at Lilo's in Seligman for lunch on Monday after hiking out of the canyon.

What's the deal with drinking water at the campground now?

The campground has a spring. The chorinator was washed away in a flood. The tribes says that all water should be treated or filtered.  (Members of our group have drunk directly from the spring with no problems later, but the Indians recommend not doing that.)

There's a drinking water faucet outside the Tourist Office in Supai (about two miles from the campground), too.

How much water should I bring for hiking in and out of the canyon?

I recommend two liters or quarts for the hike down and three liters or quarts for the hike out of the canyon.  You might want to bring some extra water on the way out to pour over your head if you expect to be hiking up the switchbacks during a sunny or hot time of day. The switchbacks can be brutally hot and shadeless.

What have you found to be effective for water treatment on the Havasupai trip?

CDC Guide to Drinking Water Treatment and Sanitation for Backcountry and Travel Use: As of summer 2012, the short story from the CDC link is that boiling water is best (rolling boil for one minute up to 6000 ft.), next best is filtering AND treating, and there aren't sufficient tests for the SteriPEN.

I bring a water filter to use both in camp and while hiking in the canyon. (My filter is an old SweetWater.) I also bring chlorine dioxide tablets to treat filtered non-spring water, since filtration is not effective in removing viruses (although I often don't use the tablets).

Recently, I've started using a SteriPEN in camp, too, but on the past couple trips the creek water below Mooney Falls has been too turbid to use the pen.

Some people drink directly from the spring at the campground, and I haven't heard of any problems from that, but the tribe recommends NOT doing that.

Of course, if you're cooking anyway, you can always boil the cooking water. Bring it to a rolling boil for at least one minute.

Some people bring iodine water purification tablets (and vitamin C to improve the taste), but most people don't want to deal with the taste.

By the way, I am more often seeing the theory that backpackers blame illness on contaminated water when really trail hygiene (due to its difficulty while hiking) was at fault. Wash your hands often! Some say that, to not get sick while on backpacking trips, you should practice better hygiene, keep all your own eating stuff (food and utensils) clean, and don't let anyone else ever touch ANY of it.

Rim Camp: lat=36.056264, lon=-112.690137

Where is the trailhead?

Follow the directions to the campsite, except drive all the way to the end of Tribal Road 18.  The parking lot, the trailer where packhorse bags should be checked in, and the trailhead are all at the very end of the road.

Isn't it hot?

I plan this trip for mid-May so that we can enjoy playing in the cool water.  The hike out of the canyon will likely be hot, though.

Is there any need for a harness or other climbing gear for this trip?

No, rock climbing is one of the activities not allowed, and we just monkey-man up and down the rope to get to Beaver Falls.  One year someone not in our group fell at the rope drop and was pretty badly hurt (and he waited many hours for evacuation).  It is not to be taken lightly!

Are there trees where we can hang our packs?

Yes, and the squirrels are very, very agressive and have lots of practice at acquiring camp food.  Even hanging bags are attacked.  Whenever you leave camp, make it as difficult as possible for the squirrels to get to your food.  Bring something to hang your food, or bring squirrel-proof containers.  Dogs usually hang out in the campground, too.

How many pack horse spots do I need?  How many packs on a horse?  How heavy can each of those packs be?

If you're a backpacker, you don't need any packhorse spots, but you wouldn't be asking if you were a backpacker.  I recommend one spot per person.  If you're completely new to this, you'll probably have to work really hard at getting all your stuff into one 32-pound packhorse bag and your hiking day pack.

One year some of our pack horse bags were not picked up from the campground, and a camera was missing when the bags were delivered days later.  To make it harder for any bags to be left behind, keep all our bags in one, big pile.

How can I make helicopter reservations?

I don't think you can make reservations.  The morning you want to leave, if the helicopter is flying, you sign up and wait for your turn.  However, other people, like tribe members, get to fly first.  It's a very quick flight, not a sight-seeing flight.  Note that sometimes the helicopter is unexpectedly grounded for maintenance or high winds.
